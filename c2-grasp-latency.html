C2: from a 50-second wait to three six-second trials

Three robot-side timings: 6.71, 6.25 and 6.43 seconds. Reuse computation, simplify small-contour distance evaluation, and retain full collision checks.

From a working grasp to a smoother experiment loop

The previous review covered successful grasps at multiple positions and a manually observed wait reduced from about 50 seconds to about 13. Today I kept the same workflow: click Start in the web panel, acquire an image, locate the cube, prepare and check the path, then execute the sequence. Three onsite trials made the shorter wait tangible.

Robot-side timestamps now give us a clearer measurement. Task-process entry to the first motion command took 6.71, 6.25 and 6.43 seconds, with a median of 6.43 seconds. Moving the cube, trying again and observing the result feels much more continuous.

Define the clock before comparing the numbers

The historical estimates of about 50 and 13 seconds were manual observations from a click to visible movement. The precise values here use the robot’s monotonic clock, from task-process entry to the first motion command. They do not include all browser-to-process delay or precisely measure physical motion onset.

With that software boundary, we first recorded 14.13 seconds, then 8.08 after reusing initialization and resident computation, and now 6.25–6.71 across three trials. Target positions and runtime load differed between trials. These are engineering observations, not a benchmark with every variable controlled.

The wait was not all path solving

It was tempting to blame planning and collision checking. Breaking down the actual execution path revealed repeated module imports, per-task preparation startup and repeated setup of the same numerical environment.

We reused the existing resident process for FK, depth validation and goal construction. Each task still uses fresh measured joints and a fresh image, and computes its own transform and trajectory. What persists is the computational environment, not a previous target or result.

That earlier group of changes reduced the same software interval from 14.13 to 8.08 seconds. A closer look at stage 5 then exposed two smaller opportunities.

Two small changes: fewer imports, fewer rebuilt trees

The outer preparation process loaded NumPy each round merely to calculate ranges, medians and offsets for one or three 3D results. Standard-library operations now handle that small job. Vision, IK and collision calculations still use their numerical libraries in the resident process.

Visual localization tries five initial orientations and repeatedly compares a projected cube silhouette with the observed contour. This image required roughly 2,600 distance evaluations. Rebuilding a tiny search tree each time cost more than directly evaluating all point-pair distances for a small contour. Large contours keep the original tree method.

The five initial orientations, contour samples, optimizer precision and fit acceptance threshold remain unchanged. The optimization reduces computation overhead without reducing the reviewed path or the localization search.

Check equivalence offline, then measure on hardware

We compared the combined changes with the deployed baseline using two saved real tasks: eight complete preparation runs in total. Localization coordinates, orientation, depth decisions, target poses and final grasp, release and home trajectories matched exactly. Every complete collision check passed. Installation and byte-exact rollback were rehearsed offline.

On the computer, median stage-5 preparation for the same saved input fell from 1.42 to 0.82 seconds. That measures offline preparation only. It is neither the full robot wait nor a claim that the robot is ready in 0.82 seconds.

After deployment, no-motion replay on the Raspberry Pi took 2.83, 2.68 and 2.83 seconds for the same saved input. I then initiated the actual trials through the web panel to obtain the three real execution timings below.

Three trials, with clear evidence boundaries

I completed three onsite trials. All three logs preserve the first motion command and a continuous sequence of unique motion-command numbers. Trials one and three also preserve successful final measured-joint verification. The second archive ends at command-sequence completion and lacks the final joint-verification record; it is not counted as a third complete endpoint-verification record.

Whether the cube was held securely is an onsite observation. Whether commands were dispatched and measured joints passed final verification comes from software evidence. Those are related but distinct. These changes reduce preparation latency; they do not increase the arm’s movement speed.

Not one second yet, but the next steps are measurable

The latest first trial prepared in 6.71 seconds, including 3.50 seconds for localization, planning and full collision validation. Task entry to the observed final result took about 41.18 seconds, including the complete arm sequence and verification. A six-second preparation does not mean a six-second grasp task.

The useful lesson was to measure one real execution chain and then remove repeated work. Full collision checks, measured starting joints, joint limits, STOP, the watchdog, communication deadlines and final feedback remain in place.

For now, I am keeping this field-tested version. Further changes should follow the logs rather than adding another elaborate architecture to chase an attractive number.
