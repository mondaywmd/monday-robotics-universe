Why I built a browser-based GS previs tool

Testing a blocking idea should not start with a long rendering wait. I wanted to move the camera, place rough geometry and leave notes directly inside a Gaussian Splat environment.

Watch the demo ↓

I wanted to block an idea, not wait for a renderer

I come from VFX. Moving a camera, placing a rough object, testing a beat and leaving a note are ordinary parts of figuring out a shot. But when I started working with Gaussian Splat environments, those ordinary tasks became surprisingly awkward.

In the Blender GS rendering workflow I was using, CPU-side splat sorting made repeated camera iteration and frame export too slow for early previs. That is a problem when I do not yet know whether the shot works. I want to see an idea, change it and keep moving.

The World Labs viewer and the open-source browser GS viewers I had tried were useful for looking around a reconstruction. I could not find a ready-to-use tool that combined guided geometry, camera keyframes and director’s notes in the way I needed for previs. For someone used to VFX tools, this was painful enough that I decided to make my own.

A small browser tool for spatial intent

I built a custom GS previs tool with Three.js and Spark. It loads a Gaussian Splat environment and gives me the few controls I need to start shaping a scene:

  • Move the camera, save keyframes and play the camera move back.
  • Place simple proxy geometry to guide layout and scale.
  • Animate proxies without first building the final assets.
  • Attach notes to particular moments and export a preview.
  • Save camera transforms, object transforms, timing and notes together as structured scene data.

Show the blocking. Give the agent something concrete.

The video communicates the idea to a person. The saved scene data gives an agent a concrete starting point. Rather than writing endless prompts about where objects should go, I can make those spatial decisions directly.

That layout can then guide downstream work through MCP and Blender: prepare an environment, refine collision geometry, replace proxies with simulation-ready assets, and place the robot and props in an Isaac Sim scene. This is a handoff of intent, not a claim that every proxy becomes a production asset automatically.

Fast enough to try another idea

This playground began with a panorama shot on an older camera. From generating the environment to making this previs took less than ten minutes in this example. The reconstruction is imperfect, but it gives me enough context to test framing, movement and timing.

A separate five-second, background-only Spark benchmark at 1920 × 1080 produced roughly 60 drawing callbacks per second and about 29 recorded frames per second on this machine. That encouraged the browser approach; it is not evidence of frame-exact capture or a finished mixed GS/mesh rendering pipeline.

The workflow I want is simple: capture during a walk, block out cameras and props over a coffee, then hand the layout back to the production tools. A phone-friendly workflow is the direction, rather than a claim that the whole simulation runs on an iPhone.

Previs does not decide how the robot will move

The short proxy animation expresses an idea. It does not tell me a real robot’s speed, whether a route is reachable, or whether an obstacle can be avoided. Those questions belong in simulation. The camera does not need to constrain the duration of the robot’s task.

I still want a DCC’s precision downstream. At the beginning, I want to spend more time shaping the idea and less time setting up a scene or describing every position. Direct manipulation for humans. Structured intent for agents.
