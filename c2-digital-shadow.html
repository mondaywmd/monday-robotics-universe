2026.10.14 / C2 · CAMERA CALIBRATION

Calibrating C2: from a printed ChArUco board to a level floor

Print an A4 calibration board, fix it to the floor, confirm real joint movement, then capture images, investigate errors and validate a local orientation correction.

2026.10.14 · Keeping the real floor level in robot coordinates

Today I returned from the digital shadow to the real C2. The camera can point down, but a horizontal floor should remain level after conversion into robot coordinates. An A4 ChArUco board, actual arm movements and synchronized camera data made that discrepancy measurable.

Why calibration began: the supplied model did not match the physical observations

This experiment did not start with a desire to print a calibration board. On October 13, we used the manufacturer-supplied URDF together with ROS joint states and coordinate transforms to estimate camera pose and observed object positions. Comparison with the physical setup exposed discrepancies: the real floor appeared tilted in robot coordinates, and the camera mounting relationship needed checking. Receiving ROS data and making the model follow joint changes did not establish accurate spatial estimates.

After finding the discrepancy, we discussed asking the manufacturer whether there was a matching compensation algorithm, a more accurate URDF, or more complete camera-mount and joint parameters. I then chose to investigate the real robot ourselves through reverse engineering. By that evening, the discussion had converged on a practical experiment: print a ChArUco board, use observations of its known geometry as an independent reference, and measure both the model error and the effect of a correction. These were options discussed; the manufacturer had not thereby supplied a new algorithm or model.

“Reverse engineering” here means identifying differences between the model and the robot's observed response. The ChArUco board does not remove error automatically. It provides a repeatable reference for estimating a correction and testing whether that correction improves new poses.

The October 14 experiment therefore introduced an independent visual reference. A printed ChArUco board with known dimensions was fixed to the floor and observed at several real arm poses. We compared orientations predicted from the supplied model and ROS data with orientations measured from images, then checked the correction at poses excluded from fitting.

The discrepancy concerns the complete model-to-hardware conversion chain; it does not prove that the entire URDF is wrong or that ROS mathematics is faulty. Camera mounting differences, reported joint values that differ from physical angles, and direction-dependent mechanical response can all contribute. The current result validates local orientation compensation, without uniquely separating those causes or completing absolute object-position calibration.

1. Print a ChArUco board to make the error measurable

The physical floor was level, yet the original camera and joint model made it appear tilted in robot coordinates. The experiment targeted that real-camera coordinate conversion.

I generated a print-ready A4 board and a separate parameter configuration: 210 × 297 mm paper, 5 × 7 squares, 35 mm square length, 25 mm marker length, DICT_5X5_100 and legacyPattern=false. The printing instruction was actual size, 100%, without fit-to-page scaling. The printed board supplied known geometry for camera observations.

2. Fix it to the floor and restart the reference

The paper initially rested on a book about 5 mm thick. It was then taped directly to the floor in a different position. This required a fresh board reference: earlier absolute poses could not be mixed with the new segment. The old segment remained useful for relative joint-response analysis. The paper needed to stay flat and stationary during capture. The level reference assumes a flat floor and an upright chassis Z axis; it was not measured with a precision level.

3. Confirm physical control, then collect calibration poses

Early commands changed reported angles without visible movement, so a sent command was not accepted as evidence of motion. After investigating the control chain, the user visually confirmed movement and return for Joint 0–3. Low-speed single-joint and combined poses then supplied data: announce motion, stop and settle at the target, record reported joint values and ChArUco images, and return to the reference. This sampled different amplitudes and combinations rather than exhaustively testing every angle.

A delayed preview also made one return appear incorrect. Stable, fresh images were needed before judging the return; an old preview was insufficient.

4. More than a fixed camera mounting angle

Individual joint tests confirmed real movement, but the same reported angle did not always produce the same physical pose. Approaching one Joint 2 target from opposite directions produced about 2.55° and 8.32 mm of camera-pose difference. That supports direction or history dependence without uniquely identifying backlash, servo deadband or mechanical compliance.

A fixed gain failed on small movements: a roughly 4° Joint 1/2 combination still left 3.30° of floor tilt. That failed sample was explicitly moved into the training set for a local piecewise response model with a prescribed approach direction. Fresh 5° poses then provided independent checks.

5. Independent checks on new poses

  • Joint 1 at about −5°: floor tilt fell from 17.59° to 0.31°.
  • Joint 1 and 2 at about −5° each: from 20.45° to 0.57°.
  • Joint 0 at about +6° with Joint 3 at about −6°: an earlier independent combination check fell from 18.09° to 0.14°.

Predictions were saved before their corresponding captures; validation observations were not used to refit those predictions. These are visual floor-orientation errors at tested poses, not workspace-wide positioning or grasping accuracy.

6. Removing transfer delays from the motion path

Reconnecting and restarting capture for every step, then waiting for large images before movement, made observation frustrating. A persistent connection now accepts small commands, stores compressed captures on the robot and retrieves them separately. A roughly 1.6 MB transfer that took 131 seconds in one sequential-read test took about 0.5–9.4 seconds with prefetching. Network variation remains. Movements still take six seconds, with an observation notice before each step.

This is a local orientation correction. Only specific poses and approach directions have been validated. Full arbitrary-pose FK, camera translation/height, absolute yaw and live integration remain unfinished. The default tool rejects unknown approach histories and unvalidated poses. Original URDF and motion parameters were preserved; the robot returned and stopped after testing.

7. In plain language: what did we actually do?

The goal throughout was simple: a physically level floor should remain level after conversion into robot coordinates. A camera observes an object relative to itself. To use that observation, the robot must combine joint angles and the camera mounting relationship to express it in its own coordinates. The original conversion had an error.

The printed ChArUco board acted as a ruler with known geometry. Fixing it to the floor and moving the arm let us compare the predicted camera orientation with the orientation measured from images. Motion direction also affected the observed pose, so one fixed mounting-angle correction was insufficient.

A local correction reduced roughly 18°–20° of floor tilt to about 0.1°–0.6° at the tested poses. This improves the calculation; it does not change the mechanism. Better orientation does not establish calibrated object positions, distances or grasping accuracy.

8. While charging: integrate the correction and replay saved data

The next step used no robot motion. The new orientation adapter was connected to the actual object-geometry calculation entry point. Saved raw images were decoded, ChArUco corners detected again and board normals estimated, then converted through the new path. Corresponding depth records exercised the same object-direction entry point. This checks that the integration reproduces the earlier experiment.

  • 9 image frames and 9 depth frames across three recorded poses passed. The maximum per-frame floor tilt was 0.576°.
  • The calibration parameters remained unchanged, with no refitting. This is a replay of existing validation records, not a new independent physical experiment.
  • Six rejection checks passed: unknown history, reverse/unverified history, unsynchronized timestamps, invalid time, an unvalidated pose and a zero direction vector.
  • The new result contains directions only. Existing position estimates are not relabeled as calibrated coordinates, and the two corrections are not stacked.

Code integration and offline replay are complete; live activation is not. No robot connection, motion command or live detector restart occurred. Without trustworthy approach history, the live entry point defaults to “new orientation correction not applied.” Next comes frame-associated motion-history integration and a small physical check announced to the user in advance. Camera translation, absolute yaw and full arbitrary-pose FK remain uncalibrated.

2026.10.13 · Seeing the real environment and aligning the model

Live LiDAR, color and depth data now appear in a read-only viewer. Object detection is running on the camera images, with a paper bag among the test targets. These results do not trigger robot movement.

Photos of the real robot revealed differences in the camera bracket and mounting direction compared with the supplied model. Recorded measurements produced a first candidate angle and position correction. This remains an offline fit, not an Isaac Sim or physical grasping validation.

Next: align the virtual robot with the floor, apply a shared camera correction, and check localization against real targets. The goal remains finding and approaching larger litter, with improvements made step by step.

2026.10.12 · First connection

I brought the manufacturer’s C2 robot model into Isaac Sim and adapted it to match my robot without a screen. Using the robot’s live state, I connected its movement to the digital model and checked forward motion, turning and all six arm joints.

This is a first digital shadow: the model follows the real robot. Two-way control and validated physics are still ahead.

Next steps at that stage

Check connection stability, bring LiDAR and depth-camera data into the view, and continue investigating a ROS2 node-discovery issue. The existing state-reading connection worked for today’s synchronization test.
