From GS previs to robot patrol — and a video made with agents

The last piece of trash was actually hard to spot. This experiment connects a creative browser layout to a measured robot test, then turns the recording into a readable technical film.

Yesterday’s blocking became today’s test scene

The browser previs gave me the camera idea and an approximate robot-and-litter layout. Today I wanted to take that intent into Isaac Sim, run the existing obstacle-avoidance and trash-patrol modules, and then show the result. This was an evaluation of an existing Gazebo-developed PPO policy, not a new training run.

I brought the playground Gaussian Splat into Isaac Sim as a native ParticleField and reused the collision environment and stable floor for physics. A Python script placed the USD assets using the test layout, asset bounds and ground alignment. The layout deliberately adds detours; it is fixed in this run. Rule-based randomization and parallel seeded runs remain next steps.

There are six obstacles: my chair, dumbbell and shoes, plus a cargo pallet, KLT bin and dolly from the NVIDIA Isaac Sim asset library. The custom asset workflow uses phone LiDAR scans captured at home and agent/MCP preparation. The mission targets are a milk jug, a water bottle and six paper wads, including larger versions for visibility.

Getting GS to display was only the first problem

At first the GS viewport looked almost white. My Houdini experience was useful here: World Labs data should keep its existing appearance rather than having spherical harmonics rebuilt blindly. We preserved the source’s degree-zero color data, checked the native schema and renderer setup, and used Fabric for the working Isaac display. I would not reduce every white-GS failure to one universal cause.

Stage metadata mattered too. A replay layer had omitted meters-per-unit and Z-up, so the world grid looked wrong. Fixing the metadata did not require rotating the robot trajectory. The GS surface also has thickness and imperfect ground height; translating its root to Z = 0 does not make every visible ground point physically flat.

The important separation: GS appearance is not the collision surface. Physics uses the environment collider and stable floor. Moving the GS for presentation should not silently change the physics setup.

Why the robot suddenly thought the route was blocked

A visible GS also contributes to rendered depth. Its noisy ground and surface thickness introduced many false obstacle cells into the depth map. In the controlled start-view comparison, the hidden-GS case had two obstacle cells; the visible-GS case had about 2,560 and blocked the route. The physical collider had not changed.

We checked visible, hidden and removed-GS cases and tested progressively lowering the GS. The useful lesson was not to pick one permanent height offset for an imperfect reconstruction. For this run, we kept GS out of the sensor/navigation render and restored it for the recorded presentation.

Fresh five-second navigation tests with GS removed and GS loaded but hidden took about 12.55 and 12.44 seconds respectively. That did not reproduce the earlier severe slowdown. A separate static-render comparison did show extra cost when GS was visible. These are small controlled probes, not a universal performance benchmark.

Reuse the policy; add depth for low obstacles

The obstacle-avoidance policy came from Gazebo and was ported into Isaac Sim. LiDAR supports navigation, while added depth obstacle mapping helps detect low objects below the LiDAR scan plane. PPO commands are checked against an inflated map and an A* route, including fallback actions, with a 12 cm body clearance and 2 cm planning margin.

YOLOE runs on the robot RGB camera with text prompts: “milk bottle with handle”, “plastic water bottle” and “crumpled tissue paper”. Depth localizes detections. After multi-frame confirmation, the target location is remembered and a reachable parking point is chosen. A later missed detection does not erase the target or switch off collision avoidance.

At the parking point, the robot stops and requests mock collection. Only a successful acknowledgement hides the corresponding simulated litter. Scene identity stays inside that mock actuator; it is not used to provide navigation target coordinates. Robot localization in this experiment still uses simulator ground truth.

The result was 8/8 mock collections in 183.25 simulated seconds, with a maximum sampled contact force of 0 N. Four of five patrol waypoints completed before the collection demo stopped. This is not physical grasping, full patrol coverage, or proof of real-world autonomy. There were imperfect detections and rejected candidates along the way.

Simulation first. Decide how to show it afterwards.

The original browser camera animation lasted five seconds. The robot task lasted much longer. I kept those separate: record the physics trajectory and collection events, then animate a presentation camera in Isaac Sim using keyframes and the Curve Editor.

I chose framing that avoids the worst GS reconstruction regions. We reduced the scene lights by half a stop so the props sit closer to the background brightness. Motion blur was already disabled; the remaining robot smear appeared to involve temporal reconstruction. Responsive denoising, GS depth/albedo settings and extra settling updates improved the captured frames.

The clean 1920 × 1080 scene pass contained 1,833 sampled frames and took about 18 minutes 2 seconds to capture, averaging 1.69 fps with 24 settling updates per frame. The robot simulation took about 6 minutes 53 seconds wall time. These numbers include the capture/inference workflow; neither is a hardware real-time benchmark.

The final video was “vibed” through drawing and notes

I did not open a dedicated compositing application. I drew boxes on screenshots and wrote instructions: put the robot view on the left, keep the director view large, add detector labels, show depth and the navigation map, make the cards more transparent, and explain the current task in English.

The agent translated those notes into a Python compositor using Pillow, NumPy, OpenCV, imageio-ffmpeg and FFmpeg. Bounding boxes and confidence values come from the actual online YOLOE output. The robot view and depth are recorded sensor samples, held between observations; the map is the saved fused obstacle map, not a decorative LiDAR animation. Both sides use the same simulated timeline at 3× playback.

Small panels show the obstacle assets and mission targets, with collection counts driven by real acknowledgement times. Short text cards explain the asset workflow and policy provenance. A project tech-stack end card closes the video. Most of the pipeline, from asset preparation to video composition, was built and automated with AI agents; I still made the framing and presentation decisions.

What I want to test next

More real-world environments, procedural layout randomization and parallel Isaac Sim scenarios. Then a robotic arm for actual manipulation, and VLA experiments for more general language-driven tasks. The current result is a useful bridge from a creative layout to a measurable simulation and an understandable presentation.
